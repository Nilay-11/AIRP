\begin{abstract}
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death, yet it remains unclear whether deep learning improves CVD classification over classical machine learning on structured clinical data. Using four cycles of the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2011--2018; $n = 21{,}552$ adults, 2{,}369 with CVD), we compared four classical models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, XGBoost) with three deep learning models (multilayer perceptron, tabular ResNet, FT-Transformer) using 20 demographic, clinical and laboratory features. Models were evaluated for discrimination (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, DeLong test), calibration (Brier score, expected calibration error) and computational cost, and interpreted with SHAP. All models performed similarly (ROC-AUC 0.837--0.848). XGBoost achieved the highest AUC, and no deep learning model significantly outperformed any classical model, although the FT-Transformer required 119 times longer tuning than XGBoost. Age was the most influential feature for every model. Feature rankings were broadly concordant (Kendall's $W = 0.75$) but clustered by the shape of the decision function, with the deep models agreeing closely with each other ($W = 0.92$). Inverse attributions for cholesterol reflected treatment after diagnosis, indicating association rather than causation. For structured health survey data, deep learning offered no measurable advantage over classical machine learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{ardiovascular} diseases (CVDs) are a major public health concern associated with demographic, physiological, biochemical, and behavioral factors~\cite{tsao2023,roth2020}. Clinical variables such as age, blood pressure, cholesterol, glucose concentration, and body mass index provide valuable information for investigating cardiovascular health and underpin established risk scores~\cite{dagostino2008,goff2014}. However, relationships among these variables can be nonlinear and interdependent, motivating the application of data-driven predictive methods.

Classical machine learning algorithms, including Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, Random Forests, and gradient-boosted decision trees, are widely applicable to structured clinical data. These methods offer different trade-offs between interpretability, computational efficiency, and the ability to capture nonlinear relationships. Deep learning models, from multilayer perceptrons to Transformer architectures designed for tabular data~\cite{gorishniy2021}, can learn complex feature interactions but may require careful regularization and sufficient training data. Their greater complexity does not necessarily guarantee superior performance on tabular health datasets~\cite{christodoulou2019,grinsztajn2022,shwartzziv2022}.

Model evaluation in clinical applications should extend beyond predictive accuracy. Identifying which features influence predictions, determining whether their contributions remain stable across algorithms, and assessing probability calibration are important for evaluating model reliability~\cite{vancalster2019}. Explainable artificial intelligence techniques, including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)~\cite{lundberg2017}, provide a framework for examining feature contributions. However, model attribution indicates predictive influence rather than causation~\cite{rudin2019}.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), conducted by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, combines health interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory measurements~\cite{nhanes}. Integrating four survey periods provides an opportunity to compare predictive models using population-level clinical data. Such integration requires consistent variable definitions, appropriate handling of missing data, and consideration of survey design and changes in data collection~\cite{johnson2013}.

This study compares four classical machine learning models with three deep learning models for cardiovascular disease classification using harmonized NHANES data. It evaluates predictive performance, calibration, and computational efficiency while examining clinical feature contributions through SHAP and feature-ablation experiments. The study further investigates whether different model families identify consistent influential features, providing a comparative assessment of predictive effectiveness and interpretability on structured clinical data.

\subsection{Related Work}
% TODO: review and edit. Facts about each study are taken from the cited papers.
Machine learning for CVD risk has been studied in large cohorts. Weng \textit{et al.}~\cite{weng2017} compared logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks against the ACC/AHA risk score in 378{,}256 UK primary-care patients; the neural network exceeded logistic regression by only 0.004 AUC. Alaa \textit{et al.}~\cite{alaa2019} showed that an automated machine-learning ensemble outperformed the Framingham score in 423{,}604 UK Biobank participants, largely by using many more variables, and Ambale-Venkatesh \textit{et al.}~\cite{ambale2017} applied random survival forests to the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis. A meta-analysis of 103 cohorts~\cite{krittanawong2020} found overlapping performance across algorithm families and substantial heterogeneity between studies.

Several studies have used NHANES. Dinh \textit{et al.}~\cite{dinh2019} reported an ensemble AUC of 0.831 for CVD using 131 variables. Martin-Morales \textit{et al.}~\cite{martinmorales2023} predicted CVD mortality and identified age and systolic blood pressure as key SHAP features, and Ahiduzzaman and Hasan~\cite{ahiduzzaman2025} compared five classical models with SHAP and LIME on NHANES 2017--2023. None of these studies evaluated deep learning models, and none measured whether attributions agree across models or remain stable across refits.

Broader evidence suggests that machine learning rarely outperforms logistic regression in clinical prediction~\cite{christodoulou2019} and that tree ensembles often match or exceed deep learning on medium-sized tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022,shwartzziv2022}. Explanation methods can also disagree with one another~\cite{krishna2024}. We address these gaps by comparing classical and deep models on identical data splits, with calibration, temporal validation, and cross-model and within-model SHAP stability.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data and Cohort}
We combined four NHANES cycles (2011--2012 to 2017--2018)~\cite{nhanes}; 2019--2020 was excluded because data collection was interrupted by COVID-19. The cohort comprised adults aged $\geq 20$ years who completed the examination and had a determinable outcome ($n = 21{,}552$; 2{,}369 cases). Prevalence was 11.0\% unweighted and 8.9\% survey-weighted, using the examination weight divided by four~\cite{johnson2013}.

\subsection{Outcome}
CVD was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack or stroke (items MCQ160B--F). Participants answering ``no'' to all five were controls; those with missing or unknown answers were excluded ($n = 94$).

\subsection{Features and Preprocessing}
Twenty features were harmonized across cycles: age, sex, race/ethnicity (four indicators), income-to-poverty ratio, education, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mean of up to three readings), pulse rate, BMI, waist circumference, total and HDL cholesterol, HbA1c, serum creatinine, uric acid, smoking status and diagnosed diabetes. Medication variables were excluded because treatment frequently follows a CVD diagnosis and would leak the label. Missingness was at most 9.8\%. Median imputation and standardization were fitted inside each pipeline on training folds only.

\subsection{Models and Training}
Data were split 80/20 with stratification (training $n = 17{,}241$; test $n = 4{,}311$). Hyperparameters were tuned by five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set to maximize ROC-AUC. The classical models were L2-regularized Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF)~\cite{breiman2001}, an RBF-kernel SVM~\cite{cortes1995} with Platt-scaled probabilities~\cite{platt1999}, and XGBoost (XGB)~\cite{chen2016} tuned by random search~\cite{bergstra2012}. The deep models were a multilayer perceptron (MLP; Linear--BatchNorm--ReLU--Dropout blocks~\cite{ioffe2015,srivastava2014}), a tabular ResNet and an FT-Transformer~\cite{gorishniy2021}, which tokenizes each feature and applies multi-head self-attention. All three were trained with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019}, binary cross-entropy and early stopping on a 15\% validation split; the selected networks had 3{,}649, 68{,}353 and 69{,}589 parameters, respectively. For ResNet and FT-Transformer the best configurations lay on a plateau (top configurations within 0.003 CV-AUC, below the CV standard deviation). Models were implemented in scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}, XGBoost and PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019}. Each model's decision threshold maximized F1 on out-of-fold training predictions.

\subsection{Evaluation}
On the test set we report ROC-AUC with bootstrap confidence intervals, PR-AUC~\cite{saito2015}, precision, recall, F1, Brier score~\cite{brier1950}, expected calibration error (ECE)~\cite{naeini2015} and calibration slope~\cite{vancalster2019}. Paired AUC differences were tested with DeLong's method~\cite{delong1988,sun2014}. Robustness was assessed over five random splits, by temporal validation (train 2011--2016, test 2017--2018), and with survey-weighted AUC.

\subsection{Explainability and Stability}
The same model-agnostic permutation SHAP estimator~\cite{lundberg2017} was applied to every model's predicted probability (300 test rows, 50 background rows); its rankings agreed with exact TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2020} for XGBoost ($\rho = 0.989$). Cross-model agreement used pairwise Spearman correlation of mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ and Kendall's $W$~\cite{kendall1939}; within-model stability used five bootstrap refits. Feature-group ablation removed one of eight groups at a time and averaged the AUC change over five splits. All final models and timings used a CPU; FT-Transformer refits for ablation and stability used a GPU.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Predictive Performance}
Table~\ref{tab:perf} and Fig.~\ref{fig:roc} summarize test performance. All seven models fell within 0.011 AUC. XGBoost was significantly better than LR ($p = 0.005$), RF ($p = 0.001$), SVM ($p = 0.002$) and ResNet ($p = 0.002$), but not than the MLP ($p = 0.064$) or FT-Transformer ($p = 0.075$). No deep model was significantly better than any classical model ($p \geq 0.161$ wherever a deep model had the higher AUC). All models were well calibrated; FT-Transformer had the slope closest to one (0.99), while ResNet and MLP were slightly over-confident.

\subsection{Robustness and Efficiency}
Across five random splits, XGBoost had the highest mean AUC (0.846), closely followed by FT-Transformer (0.845), and all models differed by about one standard deviation or less; under temporal validation all seven converged to 0.837--0.840 (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Cost differed by orders of magnitude: FT-Transformer took 119 times longer to tune than XGBoost and about 5{,}000 times longer to fit than LR.

\subsection{Feature Attributions and Stability}
Age was the most important feature for every model (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}). Overall agreement was high (Kendall's $W = 0.752$), but it followed the shape of the decision function rather than the classical/deep label (Table~\ref{tab:stab}). The tree ensembles agreed closely with each other (RF--XGB $\rho = 0.941$), while LR, SVM and the three deep models formed a second cluster (e.g., ResNet--FTT 0.935, SVM--MLP 0.886). The deep models were highly concordant ($W = 0.921$) compared with the classical models ($W = 0.742$). Tree models had the most stable full rankings across bootstrap refits, whereas FT-Transformer was the least stable.

\subsection{Feature-Group Ablation}
Removing demographic features caused the largest loss for every model (Fig.~\ref{fig:abl}; $-0.037$ to $-0.063$ AUC). Lipid, glycaemic, socioeconomic, renal and smoking groups each cost 0.001--0.007, while anthropometric and blood-pressure groups changed AUC by at most 0.003.

\section{Discussion and Summary}
Neither the MLP nor the tabular architectures (ResNet, FT-Transformer) offered a measurable advantage, consistent with benchmarks on medium-sized tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022,shwartzziv2022} and with clinical prediction studies~\cite{christodoulou2019,weng2017}. LR came within 0.009 AUC of the best model at a negligible cost~\cite{rudin2019}. Similar accuracy did not imply similar explanations: tree ensembles and smooth-boundary models distribute credit differently among correlated features, so attributions from a single model give an incomplete picture~\cite{krishna2024,hooker2021}.

Higher total cholesterol lowered predicted CVD probability in every model, and blood pressure added almost no information. Both reflect treatment after diagnosis: survey-weighted statin use was 58.0\% among cases versus 14.2\% among controls, and antihypertensive use was 62.8\% versus 20.1\%. In contrast, NHANES studies of future CVD mortality find systolic blood pressure predictive~\cite{martinmorales2023}. Because our outcome is prevalent, self-reported CVD measured cross-sectionally, the models partly learn markers of diagnosis and treatment, and attributions describe association only. Further limitations are recall bias, age top-coded at 80, and the use of survey weights for descriptive analysis rather than training. Reporting followed TRIPOD+AI~\cite{collins2024}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Summary and Future Work}
On harmonized NHANES 2011--2018 data, four classical models and three deep learning models achieved nearly identical discrimination and calibration for CVD classification; no deep model was significantly better than a classical model, while the FT-Transformer was orders of magnitude more expensive. SHAP identified age as the dominant feature for all models, but attribution agreement depended on the shape of the decision function. Future work should use incident outcomes from linked mortality data, model treatment status explicitly, incorporate the survey design into training, and evaluate ensembles of tree and deep models~\cite{shwartzziv2022}.
